## Why these three

Each is a different reading of "a tree", and each is a case the tree test
did not try (`docs/plans/agents-lessons.md`, "Not yet known").

1. **A house of people with needs** (a life game). A choice is need, then
   thing, then action. Things say what they offer, which is the declared
   room's own shape. Decisions come every few seconds, when something
   changes, so it is the tree as where an agent is: it stays at what it is
   doing until a need or an event moves it.
2. **A mystery in that house.** Built on the first: one of the people does
   something, chosen through the same choices given a reason to. What
   happened is then real and written down. Where each was is a record;
   what each saw is what was in their sight at the time (the text view,
   used as a witness's knowledge); what each says is the chat model
   speaking from their true memory, with one told to hide. Looking into it
   is an order that names nothing, where there are no words to cut the
   choices down by.
3. **A small settlement, played in turns** (a management game). Things need
   other things first. Since effects are data, plain code can look several
   steps ahead, where the engine today looks one; JEV moves each piece.
   Turns make its speed not matter.

Not a copy of any game that exists: the kinds, not anyone's rooms, people,
cards or rules.

## What each should measure

- **The house:** decisions and tokens an hour for each person; whether the
  days look sensible (nobody starves, nobody does one thing for ever, people
  differ); staying at a leaf against asking the whole question each time.
- **The mystery:** whether what witnesses say agrees with the record;
  whether the one hiding keeps their story over a whole game; whether the
  owner, and then an agent, can work it out; what a game costs.
- **The settlement:** turns to reach a goal against a simple rule that takes
  the nearest gain; what looking ahead in code adds; requests a turn.

Each runs with nothing drawn where it can, as the orders test did, and each
report ends with what it means for a world, an item, the kit or the
protocol.

## Sharpened before building (the `nswp-agents` session, agreed by the orchestrator, 2026-11-24)

- **The house: "sensible" is fixed in numbers first,** so it is not judged
  by eye: no need reaches empty; no one action takes more than about 40% of
  anyone's day; people's days differ by a stated amount. The needs are
  asked as scores and the choice of thing as a second question in the same
  request, which is another reading of the tree: both levels at once.
- **The mystery is two trials, the cheap one first.** What each person saw,
  kept as a record of what was in their sight, is plain code and is checked
  against what happened with no model at all. Whether a chat model keeps a
  story straight over a game is the separate, costly question. The looking
  experiment that had no place (can a view stand in for what a world has
  not declared) goes here: a witness knows what the view showed and no
  more.
- **The settlement: JEV chooses the goal, code finds the steps.** If code
  looks ahead and JEV only presses go, the trial tests the planner. The
  comparison is JEV choosing goals against the rule that takes the nearest
  gain, both with the same looking ahead. It can't be written without
  amounts, and probably not without two conditions at once, which are the
  format's largest gaps.
- **Memory is measured in the house and the mystery** (the owner,
  2026-11-24: "Have we proven our Nostr memory system works?"): whether a
  reason given at the start still shapes choices much later, which memories
  are shown when more exist than fit, memories a person writes for itself
  by rule (in the tab, never published), and whether a witness recalls
  rightly. No relay is needed for any of it.
- **Each trial is at least two runs,** with the cost of both said when the
  owner is asked: the tree test's answer changed on its third.

## A fourth: hide and seek, and a hunter (asked 2026-11-24)

The owner, in the `nswp-agents` session, as that session passed his words
on: "im trying to see how far we can get specific behavior out of a
general agent". He cited the Bitter Lesson, wants to "keep our options
open to enable autonomy when its marginally as good", and says code and
rules built into an agent are fine "so long as it can generalize as
well", with rules written for one game as the fall-back. He asked for
hide and seek, and a horror game's hunter, as a game to test agents on at
"different levels of intelligence".

As that session answered him, and nothing of it built: the same game and
the same skills, played by five levels of decider, from a scripted hunter
up to the chat model playing directly, so that what each level buys can
be read off one table. Its free half first (the game, the rules-only
hunter, what is in sight), as the mystery's was.

For the orchestrator's part, to hold to: the rules-only hunter is the
line every other level is read against, with the same counts; a hunter is
a fast task with choices that go stale, which is where rules beat a
decider twice on the ranges, so the levels are expected to differ most in
searching and guessing where someone hid, not in the chase; and what a
hidden player may be told about (sight, sound) is the world's to declare.
Nothing here is for the kit or a live world.
